The people stay.
The cars don’t have to.

Put those same 60 people four to a car: 15 cars instead of 60. Give them a bus carrying 60 passengers: one passenger vehicle, with a driver too. The travelers have not vanished. The vehicles have.

A city’s job is to connect people to places. Counting how many cars get through tells only part of that story.

A high-capacity transitway is a different facility from a bus sharing traffic. Stops, signals, service frequency, occupancy and safe access all matter. Space for walking and cycling also means space for trips that need no car at all.

So much energy.
So little motion.

For a conventional gasoline vehicle on the DOE/EPA combined city–highway cycle, about 18–25% of the fuel’s energy reaches the wheels. Most is lost in the engine, primarily as heat. And the wheels move the whole vehicle, along with whoever is inside.

Band widths show the 25%-to-wheels case; the remaining 75% is 100% minus 25%. Estimated combined cycle: 55% city, 45% highway. A range across vehicles, not a measurement of your trip. DOE/EPA: where the energy goes.

Electric cars change this part of the story. DOE reports 87–91% efficiency including energy recovered through regenerative braking. That vehicle-level figure does not include electricity generation or manufacturing. Electrifying a car still leaves a car to accommodate.

Occupancy changes the denominator. Energy per passenger-distance is vehicle energy divided by the people carried. A well-used bus can spread its energy use over many riders; a nearly empty one may not. Vehicle technology, route and occupancy decide the comparison. DOE’s EV explanation.

The assumptions and calculations

The baseline car gets an assumed 30 miles per US gallon (7.84 L/100 km) with one person aboard. Using DOE/EPA’s 33.7 kWh per US gallon: 33,700 ÷ 30 = 1,123.3 Wh per vehicle-mile. Divide by occupancy for energy per person-mile.

The bike model uses a 70 kg adult at an assumed 11 mph (17.702784 km/h), within Compendium activity 01020’s 10–11.9 mph category, rated at 6.8 METs. Using 1 MET ≈ 1 kcal/kg/hour: 6.8 × 70 ÷ 11 = 43.27 kcal/mile. With 1 kcal = 4,184 joules and 1 Wh = 3,600 joules, that is 50.29 Wh/mile. This is gross metabolism, not a prediction of extra food eaten. The Compendium is standardized for adults aged 19–59 and does not predict an individual’s precise expenditure.

Divide both per-mile values by 1.609344 for per-kilometer values: 698.01 Wh for the solo car and 31.25 Wh for the bike. Their ratio is about 22.34 before display rounding. Both bars begin at zero and use the same fixed scale. Changing units does not change their lengths or ratio.

Heavy metal.
Fragile people.

A moving car carries energy that has to go somewhere when it stops. A vehicle twice as heavy carries twice the energy at the same speed. Double the speed and that energy quadruples. People outside the vehicle do not have a steel cabin around them.

How the comparison works

The selected vehicle carries 120,345 foot-pounds of kinetic energy—2.25 times its energy at 20 mph. An ideal fall of 30.1 feet gives the same mass the same energy.

For the visual height reference, each story is 10 feet. Actual floor heights vary. Vehicle weight is illustrative, not a fleet average.

The energy is real.
The harm is not inevitable.

In a collision, energy can go into deformation, movement and heat. What happens to a person also depends on impact speed, vehicle shape, contact and how the collision unfolds. An energy calculation cannot predict someone’s injuries.

NHTSA links higher speeds and larger vehicles to greater dangers in crashes. Lower speeds, safer vehicles and streets designed around people matter alongside better alternatives to driving.

And before it moves,
it has to be made.

A vehicle also represents materials and manufacturing. Sharing an existing car uses the same machine for more travelers; it does not erase its production footprint. Replacing solo trips with fewer vehicles creates an opportunity to need fewer machines and less space to store them. Actual savings depend on what is built, how long it lasts and whether shared travel replaces ownership and driving.

Energy = ½ × mass × speed². Equivalent drop height = speed² ÷ (2 × gravity), using gravity ≈ 32.2 ft/s².

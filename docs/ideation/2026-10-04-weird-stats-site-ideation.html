What the ideas were judged against

Grounding Context

There is no code yet, so the ideas were checked against the original brief and against web research on prior art, data sources and what makes data experiences spread.

Topic Context

A new website (working name weird.stats) holding a large, uncategorized collection of weird, interest-piquing, "semi useful" statistics. Built by one person on a modern serverless stack (Vercel, Supabase, Neon, Railway and PostHog are already connected).

  • Firm: "a lot of weird stats"; "super sleek", "a beautiful … design that when you look at it your jaw drops", called "the other part of this", so design matters as much as the stats.
  • Soft: "semi useful, I think"; leaning away from any category ("I really don't think I do").
  • Open: which stats, how they're organized, how useful they must be. "I don't really know what else I want to do for this."
  • Named worry: "that's going to be a lot of data or API requests."
  • Tensions: random pile vs a site people can navigate and remember; weird vs useful; lots of data plus top-tier craft vs one person's time.

External Context

  • neal.fun is the solo precedent. Neal Agarwal works on it full time, funded by ads, a tip jar and a newsletter. The Deep Sea and The Size of Space turn scale into space. The guiding rule is "one strong interaction": no accounts, no onboarding.
  • Spurious Correlations used software to merge free government datasets and auto-produce charts. Because the charts were reusable, they spread to the BBC, blogs and a 2015 book.
  • Higher Lower (500M+ plays) and Wordle (~4M daily users in 2025) show that guessing before the reveal works. One Million Checkboxes reached ~500k people in two weeks for ~$850, with bandwidth capped rather than auto-scaled.
  • Worldometer's "live" counters are modeled projections, not live feeds; it makes money from ads and from licensing those counters. FiveThirtyEight's 2025 shutdown shows how fragile a data brand tied to a single parent company is.
  • Data:
    • Wikipedia pageviews: CC0, hourly since 2007.
    • Wikidata: good for offline mining, not live requests.
    • Our World in Data: CC BY, all CSVs on GitHub.
    • USGS and NASA feeds.
    • Trap: Open-Meteo's free tier is non-commercial only.
    • Nearly everything can be precomputed into static files on a CDN, which mostly removes the API worry.
  • Four shapes that spread: you vs the world (Spotify Wrapped); guess, then reveal; scale made spatial; absurd side-by-sides (Harper's Index, a "statistical poem").
  • Gaps:
    • Nobody runs a continuously refreshed weird-stats collection with one premium visual language.
    • None of the sites checked has a share image for each stat.
    • Nobody personalizes without collecting data.

Sources

  • Neal Agarwal (Wikipedia): ad-funded solo creator, project history
  • Interview with Tyler Vigen: the Spurious Correlations engine
  • The Higher Lower Game goes viral: origin and play counts
  • Scaling One Million Checkboxes: cost and bandwidth cap
  • Wikimedia robot policy: rate limits; dumps preferred

The parts of the subject every lens had to cover

Topic Axes

  1. The stats themselves: what counts as a weird-but-semi-useful stat, and where such stats come from.
  2. How one stat is shown: the visual and interaction form of a single stat.
  3. How the collection hangs together: the organizing principle and navigation for an uncategorized pile.
  4. Why people come back and share: return rituals, personalization, share artifacts.
  5. Keeping it alive solo: data refresh, cost, upkeep, and how it pays for itself.

Seven directions, ranked

Ranked Ideas

All seven, as one site

The combined site: one engine, one look, one verb, three doors

Guess the number, then fall down the rabbit hole.

The seven ideas aren't competing sites. Each one is a different layer of the same site. Behind the scenes, the Mint (I3) and the Stat Crew (I7) drop candidates into one approval queue on your phone. Anything approved becomes a Receipt (I6), and the site is built from those records into static files every night, so visitors never trigger an API call. Every record is drawn by one of the Seven Forms (I1) and opens with a guess (I2). Visitors come in through three doors: Today (I5), the Line (I4), and individual stat pages reached from shared links and search. The crowd's guesses then flow back into the engine as new stats.

The rabbit hole: three exits on every stat page

  • Same size: its neighbors on the Number Line (I4).
  • Same subject: everything else about the same thing, place or date. The Mint (I3) already links data by subject, so pages like "everything weird about Iceland" or "everything weird about October 4" come almost free.
  • Same mistake: the stats people get most wrong, from the guess data (I2).

Every exit leads to more stats with three more exits. That's how someone spends hours on the site, and none of the links are made by hand.

I1

1. Seven Forms

Design the forms once; after that, every stat is just data.

Axis
How one stat is shown
Confidence
85%
Complexity
Medium

Description

Don't design each stat. Hand-build about seven premium visual forms, each a different way to make a number felt:

  • Unit Pile: one dot per thing, with exactly one lit in a million.
  • Scale Drop: scroll to fall through orders of magnitude.
  • Rate Clock: a counter ticking at the real per-second rate.
  • Tale of the Tape: A against B.
  • You-Are-Here: a distribution with your own dot on it.
  • Rank Race: bars reshuffling over time.
  • Map Bloom: dots piling up on a map.

Each stat is then a row of data that names its shape, and the matching form draws it. The same render also produces the stat's page, its 1200×630 share image and an embed code. Each form is built in tiers:

  • A static poster first, which doubles as the share image.
  • Canvas motion next.
  • WebGL last, only on devices that can handle it and behind a kill switch.

Basis

  • directThe user calls the design "the other part of this" and wants "a lot of" stats. The grounding names "lots of data + jaw-dropping craft vs one person's time" as the core tension.
  • externalSpurious Correlations spread because software produced templated, reusable charts. Sports TV shows hundreds of unrelated stats through one prebuilt graphics package. neal.fun's rule is "one strong interaction" per piece.
  • reasonedIf the craft lives in the form rather than the stat, polish is paid for once, and stat #400 costs a few minutes of data entry.

Rationale

Four of the five idea agents reached this independently, from different directions. It's the one decision that makes "jaw-dropping" and "a lot" compatible for one person. It also gives the whole site a single visual voice instead of a scrapbook look. Per-stat share images come free, and the research found no weird-stats site that has them.

Downsides

  • Seven forms with three tiers each is a lot of upfront work. Prove two or three forms first and leave WebGL for later.
  • A stat that fits no form gets cut or waits for a new form, which constrains content.
  • Mediocre forms make everything feel templated. Quality has to be very high.

2. Guess First

You commit before the number appears, and the crowd's wrongness becomes a stat of its own.

Axis
How one stat is shown
Confidence
75%
Complexity
Low–Medium

Description

No stat shows its number until you guess. You guess on a log-scale slider, or by shaping the amount yourself, for example pouring a jar or dragging a ruler. When you let go:

  • The true value animates in.
  • Your guess stays on screen as a ghost.
  • Everyone else's guesses appear as a histogram.

Each guess logs one anonymous number, with no accounts. Over time every stat gains a second stat, such as "most visitors guess 20 billion; it's about 117 billion". It also gains a measured surprise score that can rank the feed. A shared link's preview shows the question with the answer hidden, so people click.

A variant for stats with a distinctive pattern: show the picture with no labels and let people guess what it is. For example, a calendar that glows in September, with cold holes on Dec 24–25, Jan 1 and Jul 4, is how common each US birthday is.

Basis

  • externalIpsos's Perils of Perception survey turned exactly this gap into a recurring press franchise. In 2017 it ran 29,133 interviews in 38 countries; Americans guessed immigrants were 33% of the population against 14% actual. Verified.
  • directGuess-then-reveal is one of the four recurring viral shapes in the research: Higher Lower (500M+ plays) and Wordle (~4M daily users in 2025). PostHog is already connected, so anonymous guesses can be logged.
  • reasonedA bare number has no stakes, and a guess creates them. The gap between guess and truth is what "weird" actually means, so the site can measure weirdness instead of claiming it.

Rationale

It turns reading into a gut-check collision with reality, which is the moment the user is after ("pique users' interests"). Every visit also produces a by-product: a dataset of misperceptions no other site has, and good press material ("weird.stats visitors think X; it's actually Y").

Downsides

  • Crowd numbers mean nothing until each stat has a few hundred guesses.
  • Self-selected visitors and bots are not a poll. Label the numbers honestly and filter out junk.
  • The slider's range anchors people's guesses.
  • Guessing on every stat gets tiring, so make it the default but skippable.
  • Link previews can show the question but not the sharer's own guess, because crawlers can't read URL fragments.
I3

3. The Mint

A nightly machine finds the weird stats, and you just approve them.

Axis
The stats themselves
Confidence
65%
Complexity
High

Description

Instead of hunting for stats by hand, a nightly job joins free datasets on shared keys: the thing (its Wikidata ID), the country and the date. It then runs "surprise templates" over the joined data:

  • Ratio extremes.
  • Rank flips: #1 on one measure, near last on another.
  • Unit coincidences: two unrelated quantities within 1% of each other.
  • Record-holders.

That produces thousands of candidates a night. An LLM writes only the caption, and a separate step recomputes every number from the source rows. You spend about 10 minutes a day on your phone swiping approve or kill.

Useful house rule: favor stats that only exist because of a join, so nobody can find them by searching. Flagship template: the Attention Gap, which compares what people read about with what actually happens. For example, the week's biggest earthquake next to the Wikipedia pageviews for the nearest town.

Basis

  • directThe research confirms the raw material is free and joinable. Wikipedia pageviews are CC0, hourly since 2007, and joinable to Wikidata. Our World in Data is CC BY, with every CSV on GitHub. Wikidata is "good for offline mining of weird superlatives".
  • directThe user's open question: "I don't really know what else I want to do", plus "a lot of random weird data".
  • externalSpurious Correlations was exactly this. Software merged free government datasets and auto-produced charts, which led to BBC coverage and a 2015 book.
  • reasonedOne person can't write 10,000 stats but can review 10 a day. That's about 3,650 curated stats a year, with no live API load.

Rationale

It answers the biggest open question ("which stats?") with a machine instead of a list. It gives "weird" a working definition: something extreme about something familiar. And it turns the worry about data volume into a nightly batch job that visitors never touch.

Downsides

  • The hardest idea here to build well, and its output quality is unknown until you try it.
  • Wikidata is incomplete, so "the only X that…" claims can be false.
  • Ratios on tiny countries such as Vatican City or Tuvalu produce junk extremes that need filtering.
  • Data-savvy audiences like Hacker News will pounce on a wrong number, which is why the recheck step and receipts (I6) matter.

4. The Number Line

No categories: the whole collection lives on one zoomable scale.

Axis
How the collection hangs together
Confidence
65%
Complexity
Medium

Description

The home page is one logarithmic number line that you scroll or zoom through, like The Deep Sea but for any quantity. It has one lane per kind of quantity: counts, time, mass, length and money. Each stat sits at its value, so neighbors share nothing but size, and that produces collisions on its own. Near 10¹¹, for example, sit ~86 billion neurons in a brain, 100–400 billion stars in the Milky Way and ~117 billion humans ever born.

A shelf of a few hundred reference objects gives every stat an "≈ N of these" comparison: a blue whale, an Olympic pool, the Eiffel Tower, all mineable from Wikidata. The shelf can include "you" units, such as your height or your hometown. Type any number, like your birth year or 42, and the line flies there.

Basis

  • direct"I really don't think I do [want a category]… I just want a lot of random weird data". Also the grounding's tension between a random pile and a site people can navigate and remember.
  • externalHarper's Index gets its power from juxtaposition and from the reader not knowing what comes next. neal.fun's The Deep Sea and The Size of Space turn scale into space. Wikidata stores quantities with units (verified).
  • reasonedSize is a property almost every stat has, so it organizes the pile without anyone having to maintain a taxonomy.

Rationale

It makes "no category" a deliberate design choice rather than a missing feature, and the navigation doubles as the jaw-drop hero visual. Typing a number personalizes the site without any account.

Downsides

  • Not every stat has a natural size. Percentages, rankings, dates and patterns need another home, such as the daily spread and the stat pages.
  • Most same-size pairs are meaningless, so the collisions still need curation.
  • Thousands of points in WebGL need careful work to run well on phones.

5. The Daily Spread

A three-card daily ritual with a spoiler-free share grid.

Axis
Why people come back and share
Confidence
60%
Complexity
Low–Medium

Description

Once a day you drag to cut a deck, and three stat cards land in named spots:

  • About You: worked out in your browser from your birth year and country, e.g. "you've lived through ~470 full moons".
  • About the World.
  • The Absurd.

You guess each card before it flips (I2). The result is a Wordle-style share grid showing how far off you were on each card (1.1×, 3×, 40×) without giving away the answers. Decks come in finite, art-directed seasons of, say, 78 cards. That makes them collectible, and they can later be printed as a physical deck.

Basis

  • externalWordle (~4M daily users in 2025) proved a fixed daily ritual with one shareable, spoiler-free artifact. Spotify Wrapped proved that sharing is identity expression. Tarot shows how a draw you take part in (you cut the deck), with named positions, makes randomness feel meaningful.
  • directThe user asked for "random weird data". The grounding names personalization without data collection (birth year and country, computed in the browser) as an unfilled gap.
  • reasonedA shuffle button feels throwaway. A ritual with named positions turns the same randomness into a small story, with a daily reason to come back.

Rationale

The user's "random" stops being a weakness and becomes the ritual. It's the one return loop that needs no accounts, notifications or live infrastructure. Seasons give one person a sustainable release rhythm, and each new deck is a fresh launch moment.

Downsides

  • Daily games risk "another Wordle clone" fatigue.
  • It needs about three good cards a day, roughly 1,100 a year, so it depends on the Mint or a deep backlog.
  • Crowd comparisons start out empty.
  • A printed deck means fulfillment work, so treat it as a later step.

6. Receipts on Every Stat

Every number shows its work, refreshes itself, and can't break a license.

Axis
Keeping it alive solo
Confidence
80%
Complexity
Low–Medium

Description

Each stat is a small record rather than a sentence. It holds:

  • The value and its unit.
  • The source and the date it was retrieved.
  • The formula or query that produced it.
  • A license class.
  • A badge: measured, modeled or estimated.
  • How often it refreshes, and how old it is allowed to get.

A nightly build re-runs everything into static files on a CDN, so visitors never hit an outside API. The build also:

  • Raises an alarm on implausible jumps.
  • Relabels stale stats "as of 2025" and pulls them from rotation.
  • Refuses to publish any stat whose source is non-commercial or has unclear terms.

On the site, flipping a card shows a designed "specimen label" with the receipt. The same machinery powers a Forgeries Room: famous fake stats, displayed the way museums display fakes. For example, "you swallow 8 spiders a year" has no source. Even the popular story that a columnist named "Lisa Birgit Holst" invented it in 1993 is fake: the name is an anagram of "this is a big troll".

Basis

  • directThe user's worry: "a lot of data or API requests". The research's traps: Open-Meteo's free tier is non-commercial only, OpenSky's commercial terms are unspecified, and Worldometer's "live" counters are models, not feeds.
  • externalOWID notes that third-party charts carry their own licenses. Museums make a heap of unrelated objects trustworthy with labels and catalog numbers. The spider-myth hoax is documented by Snopes (verified).
  • reasonedOne person can't re-check thousands of stats by hand, so upkeep has to grow with the exceptions, not with the total.

Rationale

The weird-fact genre is full of made-up numbers. In practice, "semi-useful" means you can repeat a stat at dinner without getting burned. This idea removes the API worry and the licensing risk on day one, before any stat exists. It is cheap, too: mostly a decision about what a stat record contains.

Downsides

  • The structure has to be built before the first fun thing ships.
  • The license gate rules out some tempting sources.
  • The Forgeries Room is a finite, hand-written wing. Write your own copy rather than lifting Snopes'.

7. The Stat Crew

A front page that writes itself by comparing today with history.

Axis
The stats themselves
Confidence
60%
Complexity
Medium

Description

Raw live data is dull: an earthquake count means nothing on its own. Sports broadcasts make routine moments thrilling with "first time since…" notes. An automated stat crew compares each refreshing series with its own past every night and writes record-framed sentences, such as:

  • "Today's M6.4 near Tonga is the largest quake anywhere in 23 days."
  • "'Saturn' got its most English Wikipedia views since March."
  • "X overtook Y for the first time."
  • "This stat was just corrected by −12%."

It also tracks seasonal pulses, such as Wikipedia views of "Daylight saving time" climbing before the clocks change. And it surfaces evergreen stats about whatever the internet is suddenly curious about.

Basis

  • externalThe Elias Sports Bureau has supplied "first since" notes to ESPN, Turner and the major leagues for decades (verified). Wikimedia's daily most-viewed endpoint exists and works, with a 1–2 day lag (verified).
  • directThe research names staleness as a risk and advises a small live set on top of an evergreen core. It also lists USGS feeds updated every minute and hourly Wikipedia pageviews since 2007.
  • reasonedA counter isn't a story; a number compared with its own past is.

Rationale

It gives the site a pulse and a reason to look again tomorrow, without an editor. And it turns the live data the user expected to need into a nightly batch job.

Downsides

  • The trending list is mostly deaths, adult content and bots, and it includes Main_Page and Special:Search. It needs real filtering, not just a blocklist.
  • "First since" claims need clean history, and USGS revises magnitudes after the fact.
  • Some record sentences will be boring, so they still go through the approval queue.

All five axes have at least one survivor. Several ideas weren't wrong; they were folded into a stronger survivor, and those rows say where they went.

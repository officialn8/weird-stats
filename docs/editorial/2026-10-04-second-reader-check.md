# Reader check 2 — would you actually tell someone?

Prepared October 4, 2026. Status: creator walkthrough complete for the four new candidates, including recall and continuation questions. Independent reader responses remain uncollected.

Moderator material. Sources, qualifications, and selection reasoning are in the [research notes](2026-10-04-editorial-reset.md). Do not send this entire document to a reader before testing: it contains the answers.

## Run it

Recruit 3–5 people who have not seen these candidates. The creator and anyone already shown the answers can still give useful feedback, but record their prior exposure separately. No invitations have been sent.

Say: “We're checking which discoveries are worth including. Skipping is useful feedback. You don't need to guess an answer or be polite about it.”

Show one opening at a time, without visuals, music, the source, or the editor's pitch. Ask “Reveal, or keep scrolling?” Record that as a stated choice, not an observed website click. If they skip, move on. Offer skipped reveals only after the main sequence, marking those reactions as prompted.

After each chosen reveal, leave room for a spontaneous reaction. Then ask these same questions:

1. “Did you already know the underlying idea before today?”
2. “Did anything change what you expected? What?”
3. “Would you tell someone this? If so, who—and what would you say?”

Do not supply a sample retelling. “No” is complete feedback. If an explanation is unclear, record their first interpretation before clarifying it. Mark any clarified answer separately.

After the sequence, hide the text: “Which, if any, do you remember?” Then “Would you choose to see another one?” Immediate recall and stated interest are not long-term retention or actual sharing.

Rotate order using these suggested sequences. If using fewer than five readers, use only the corresponding rows; do not invent missing participants.

## Exact copy — version 1

### WS-15 · Crunch

**Opening:** Could a volume knob make a potato chip seem fresher?

**Reveal:** Researchers changed the crunch people heard through headphones. Chips seemed crisper and fresher with enhanced sound. Afterward, 15 of 20 participants said they thought the chips came from different packages. They came from the same package. That count came from informal questions after one small experiment.

### WS-16 · Selfie

**Opening:** Same face. No filter. What changes when the camera moves closer?

**Reveal:** Its proportions in the photo. A geometry model estimated that, from 12 inches away, the nose appears about 30% wider relative to the face than in the model's undistorted reference view. The effect comes from distance; that number is a model result, not a measurement of your selfie.

### WS-17 · Ice cream

**Opening:** How much of a scoop of ice cream could be air?

**Reveal:** As much as half its volume. Air bubbles help give ice cream its texture; the amount varies.

### WS-18 · Printed page

**Opening:** Could a printed page reveal which printer made it—even without a name on it?

**Reveal:** EFF decoded nearly invisible yellow dots on Xerox DocuColor printouts. A repeating 15-by-8 grid encodes a printer serial number and a date and time. That's a finding about those printers, not every printer, and their clocks can be wrong.

### WS-02 · Copper baseline

**Opening:** Which holds more copper: a modern U.S. penny or a nickel?

**Reveal:** The nickel—about 60 times as much. This compares the standard copper-plated-zinc penny with the standard cupronickel nickel, using their specified weights and compositions.

Source and calculation: [existing WS-02 entry](first-selection.md#ws-02--the-copper-is-in-the-other-coin). This is an existing candidate used for comparison, not newly discovered material.

## Read the results

Look for repeated reasons across people. “I knew that” is a familiarity problem; “I didn't know that, but so what?” is a selection problem; an interested but inaccurate retelling may be a wording problem. A bare “yes, I'd share it” without a recipient or a retelling is weak evidence.

Choose up to two entries for visual tests, or research again if none earns it. Text screening cannot establish visual appeal. The current orange scrolling prototype remains the reference for that separate design question.

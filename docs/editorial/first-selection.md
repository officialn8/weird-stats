## Prototype shortlist

### WS-13 — A very different sleep schedule

**Question:** “An elephant seal may sleep about ten hours a day on the beach. What about at sea?”

**Reveal:** “About two hours a day on long foraging trips.”

**Explanation:** “Northern elephant seals take short naps—under 20 minutes—while diving, as deep as 377 meters. Some turn upside down and drift downward in a slow spiral. Two hours a day rivals the African elephant's record for the least sleep of any mammal.”

**Visual and interaction:** A land/sea toggle changes the setting and a labeled daily-sleep bar. The answer remains available without manipulating the control. An optional short spiral illustrates a sleeping dive; label it schematic. Use “about 10 hours” and “about 2 hours,” without implying an exact fivefold ratio.

**Evidence:** Study-based estimate. Brain-activity and dive data from 13 juvenile females, including 104 sleep dives, informed an algorithm estimating sleep from the dive records of 334 free-ranging seals (514,406 sleeping dives). The two-hour figure is an average over seven months at sea, not months of direct brain recordings from every seal. The land figure is the research team's “may spend 10 hours a day sleeping on the beach”; do not upgrade it to “over.” The abstract does not say whether the 334 were adults; confirm in the paper before describing them that way. Scope is northern elephant seals and the studied foraging context.

**Source:** [Scripps Institution of Oceanography, April 20, 2023](https://scripps.ucsd.edu/news/elephant-seals-drift-sleep-while-diving-far-below-ocean-surface), research-team account and methods summary; [Kendall-Bar and colleagues, Science, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adf0566), abstract (377 m, 334 seals, 514,406 dives, seven months, African elephant comparison). Review annually.

**Familiarity assessment:** Provisional. Covered in science news; no evidence yet that the intended readers find it new. Test recognition of the sleep change and underwater naps, not recall of the exact hours.

**Opening variant to test:** the first walkthrough found the original question lacked curiosity, so this variant draws curiosity from the mechanism. Test it alongside the original; do not assume it succeeds.
- *Question:* “Where does an elephant seal sleep when it's months from land?”
- *Reveal:* “Underwater, mid-dive—sometimes upside down, drifting down like a falling leaf. About two hours a day in all.”
- *Evidence:* “Falling leaf” is the Scripps account's description. The seals' foraging trips last months, and the abstract's average covers seven months at sea.

**Next connection:** WS-09, another long way down: a sleeping seal's spiral, then a three-hour mule descent.

### WS-02 — The copper is in the other coin

**Question:** “Which holds more copper: a modern U.S. penny or a nickel?”

**Reveal:** “The nickel. About 60 times as much.”

**Explanation:** “A penny's copper coating accounts for 2.5% of its 2.5-gram mass. A nickel is 75% copper and weighs 5 grams.”

**Visual and interaction:** Choose a coin or tap “Show me.” Reveal two proportional bars of copper mass, labeled 0.0625 g and 3.75 g. Keep the coin illustrations separate from the quantitative bars; cross-sectional area is not a mass fraction.

**Evidence:** Original calculation from Mint specifications: `(5 × 0.75) ÷ (2.5 × 0.025) = 60`. Rounded manufacturing specifications, not a measurement of two particular coins. Scope is the modern copper-plated-zinc penny and modern cupronickel nickel, not historic compositions.

**Source:** [U.S. Mint, Coin Specifications](https://www.usmint.gov/learn/coins-and-medals/circulating-coins/coin-specifications), Penny and Nickel tables. Review before publication and annually. Circulating penny production ended on November 12, 2025 ([U.S. Mint](https://www.usmint.gov/news/press-releases/united-states-mint-hosts-historic-ceremonial-strike-for-final-production-of-the-circulating-one-cent-coin)); existing pennies remain legal tender. Never imply new pennies are being made for circulation.

**Next connection:** WS-14, another case where familiar appearances hide an unexpected amount.

### WS-11 — The college that kept its own clock

**Question:** “Oxford's Great Tom rings for ‘9 p.m.’ every evening. When does it actually ring?”

**Reveal:** “At 9:05 p.m.—101 times.”

**Explanation:** “Christ Church still keeps ‘Oxford time,’ five minutes behind Greenwich. The 101 strikes trace to the college's original 100 Students, plus one added to the foundation in 1663.”

**Visual and interaction:** Two clock faces, Greenwich and Oxford, set five minutes apart. Choose “9:00” or “9:05,” or reveal immediately. After the reveal, 101 small marks grouped into tens with one extra. Optional audio could come later; never require waiting through all 101 strikes.

**Evidence:** Institutional account of a tradition. The college's visitor page describes the 9:05 p.m. ringing in the present tense (checked October 4, 2026). “Five minutes” is the college's rounded figure; Oxford's longitude, about 1.25° west, works out to five minutes of local solar time (`1.25° × 4 min/°`, our calculation). ‘Students’ is Christ Church's historical institutional term; do not translate it into today's undergraduate enrollment. Avoid implying an uninterrupted performance every evening or making this a live visitor schedule.

**Source:** [Christ Church, Things to see](https://www.chch.ox.ac.uk/visit/things-to-see), Great Tom (time and Oxford time); [Christ Church, A Brief History](https://www.chch.ox.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2023-09/ChCh-history-web.pdf), Tom Tower passage, PDF pages 9–10 (origin of the count). Review annually.

**Opening variant to test:** the first walkthrough found the original assumed readers know what Great Tom is, so this variant gives the context before the name. Test it alongside the original; do not assume it succeeds.
- *Question:* “One Oxford college rings its 9 p.m. bell by a clock of its own. How far off is it from the rest of Britain?”
- *Reveal:* “Five minutes. Great Tom rings at 9:05 Greenwich time—101 times.”
- *Evidence:* Oxford time governs the college's traditions, such as the bell. Do not imply the whole college runs on it.

**Next connection:** WS-13, another unexpected schedule. Promoted to the revised prototype shortlist; current-practice source rechecked, audience familiarity untested.

### WS-14 — A pixel's worth of paint

**Question:** “Zoom all the way into this image of Rembrandt's Night Watch. How much paint does one pixel show?”

**Reveal:** “A square just 0.005 millimeters across.”

**Explanation:** “To capture that detail, the Rijksmuseum took 8,439 photographs of the painting and stitched them into a single 717-billion-pixel image for studying it in extraordinary detail.”

**Visual and interaction:** On reveal, zoom from the whole painting to a magnified pixel-scale inset; then pull back to a mosaic showing how the 8,439 photographs fit together. Label the mosaic schematic; do not invent the actual capture grid. The museum says each input photograph covered 5.5 × 4.1 cm of the painting, so one tile can be shown at its stated size as a scale anchor. Link to the museum's real zoomable image. Before using a painting reproduction, verify the specific asset's reuse terms; an original diagram can explain the count without it. Never require loading the full-resolution composite.

**Evidence:** An imaging project the museum published on January 3, 2022 (a publication date, not necessarily when the photographs were taken). The count refers to input photographs, not camera exposures per second, unique paintings, or a single camera shot. 717 gigapixels is the final composite's reported resolution. 0.005 mm is the side of the square each pixel represents (the museum's “an area of 5 micrometres or 0.005mm square”), not the size of a brushstroke or a paint particle. Avoid a current “world's largest” claim.

**Source:** [Rijksmuseum, January 3, 2022](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/press/press-releases/rijksmuseum-publishes-717-gigapixel-photograph-of-the-night-watch), imaging-method passage. Stable historical project; review links annually.

**Familiarity assessment:** Provisional. Likely more familiar to photography and art-conservation readers; that is an editorial inference, not measured audience evidence. The entry now leads with the microscopic scale; test whether it lands on its own, with the photograph count explaining it rather than carrying it.

**Next connection:** WS-05, a small physical movement at a famous landmark.


### WS-05 — The tower follows the sunshine

**Question for browsing:** “Does the Eiffel Tower stand completely still?”

**Visible fact:** “On a clear day, its tip can trace a curve about 15 centimeters across.”

**Explanation:** “Sunlight heats the sides unevenly. The iron expands, and the tower leans slightly away from the Sun as its position changes.”

**Visual and interaction:** Display the answer immediately. Offer a replay of a sun moving around the tower and a magnified overhead trace. Mark the motion as exaggerated; a 15-centimeter scale belongs on the inset.

**Evidence:** Approximate behavior reported by the operator. The 15 cm is the diameter of the tip's roughly circular path, not a radius or a claim of 15 cm vertical growth. Do not imply a precise circle on every day. Make no claim about height change: the operator says heat makes the tower only “a few millimeters” taller, while widely shared articles claim up to 15 cm. The sideways curve is the operator's own figure and the safer claim.

**Source:** [Eiffel Tower operator, Why does the Eiffel Tower change size?](https://www.toureiffel.paris/en/news/history-and-culture/why-does-eiffel-tower-change-size), solar-heating explanation. Review annually.

**Next connection:** WS-11, another landmark with a peculiar daily routine.

### WS-09 — The mail has hooves

**Question:** “How does mail reach a village deep inside the Grand Canyon?”

**Reveal:** “By mule train—typically 10–22 mules.”

**Explanation:** “The village is Supai, home of the Havasupai people. USPS describes mule deliveries five days a week: about nine miles down, roughly three hours for the descent. It calls this the most unusual mode of delivery the Postal Service uses.”

**Visual and interaction:** Choose from illustrated modes of transport (for example truck, helicopter, drone, mule) or reveal. A short trail drawing introduces the mule train; a clear caption identifies the place and service.

**Evidence:** Operational information, explicitly “USPS, May 2026.” The current source says nine miles; older USPS material says eight. Keep the dated current account instead of combining figures across editions. Supai is in Havasu Canyon, a side canyon; say “inside” or “deep inside” the Grand Canyon, as USPS does, not “at the bottom.” “Most unusual mode of delivery” is USPS's own phrase; do not upgrade it to “the only mule route.” Treat the community and service respectfully; the delivery method supplies the surprise.

**Source:** [USPS Postal Facts, Fun facts](https://facts.usps.com/fun/), Mule train, Fact #310, May 15, 2026. Recheck before publication and every six months.

**Next connection:** WS-02, another everyday U.S. service hiding a surprise. Promoted to the revised prototype shortlist; familiarity remains to be tested.

### WS-07 — Winter on pause

**Question:** “How long did these Alaskan wood frogs stay frozen?”

**Reveal:** “About 193 consecutive days, on average—and all 18 survived.”

**Explanation:** “A study followed 18 wood frogs through an Interior Alaska winter in their natural shelters. They stayed frozen for roughly six months, through average low temperatures of about −15 °C, and every one of them thawed out alive in spring.”

**Visual and interaction:** A small frog rests beside a calendar strip. Reveal the duration across months, then let the frog move once. Keep it gentle; no ‘back from the dead’ language.

**Evidence:** Study observation, not a universal survival limit. The paper reports `193 ± 11 days`, average minimum temperatures of `−14.6 ± 2.8 °C` (range −8.9 to −18.1 °C), and `100% survival (N = 18)`; retain the study framing. Freezing tolerance does not mean every cell freezes solid. Do not turn this into instructions for handling animals.

**Source:** [Larson and colleagues, Journal of Experimental Biology, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24737762/), abstract; DOI 10.1242/jeb.101931. Review annually.

**Next connection:** WS-13, another animal whose body runs on an unexpected schedule. Alternative to a prototype entry if early readers already know it.

### WS-10 — One fungus, a forest-sized footprint

**Question:** “How much ground can one fungus occupy?”

**Reveal:** “One individual in Oregon was estimated to occupy about 9.65 square kilometers.”

**Explanation:** “A Forest Service report describes an Armillaria individual spread across 965 hectares of forest. The organism extends through soil and tree roots. The researchers who mapped it estimated it to be between 1,900 and 8,650 years old.”

**Visual and interaction:** Reveal a scale grid, then an illustrative branching network beneath trees. Label the network schematic. Do not draw an enormous mushroom cap or invent a surveyed boundary.

**Evidence:** Historical area estimate. `965 hectares ÷ 100 = 9.65 km²`. The 2005 report cites Ferguson and colleagues, 2003. No present-day size claim or ‘world's largest’ ranking is needed. The age is a model-based range from three assumed spread rates; always show it as a range, never a single number.

**Source:** [USDA Forest Service, Root Diseases in Coniferous Forests of the Inland West, 2005](https://research.fs.usda.gov/download/treesearch/20469.pdf), printed page 6, Armillaria Root Disease. Age range: [Ferguson and colleagues, Canadian Journal of Forest Research, 2003](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/20135). Stable historical claim; review annually for corrections.

**Next connection:** WS-02, another amount hidden where you wouldn't think to look.

### WS-01 — A cloud on the scales

**Question:** “How much water is hiding in this fluffy cloud?”

**Reveal:** “About 500,000 kilograms (1.1 million pounds)—in this example.”

**Explanation:** “Imagine a cumulus cloud filling a cube one kilometer across. At 0.5 grams of liquid water per cubic meter, its droplets add up to half a million kilograms.”

**Visual and interaction:** A soft cloud above a small scale; “Show me” is primary, “Take a guess” secondary. On reveal, a labeled volume and water-mass readout appear. Avoid a long count-up. An optional volume control recalculates the estimate.

**Evidence:** Illustrative estimate. `1,000³ m³ × 0.5 g/m³ ÷ 1,000 = 500,000 kg`. This is droplet mass, excluding air. Clouds differ in size and water content; never present this as the weight of every cloud. The one-cubic-kilometer assumption must appear with the answer.

**Source:** [USGS, How Much Does a Cloud Weigh?](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/how-much-does-a-cloud-weigh), July 7, 2019; calculation section. Stable example; review annually.

**Next connection:** WS-02, another familiar object whose contents are surprising.

### WS-03 — The little cube factory

**Question:** “How many cube-shaped poops does a wombat make in a day?”

**Reveal:** “Typically 80–100.”

**Explanation:** “Bare-nosed wombats produce block-shaped droppings. Researchers found that the corners take shape inside the intestine, before the droppings leave the body—work that won a 2019 Ig Nobel Prize in Physics.”

**Visual and interaction:** “Show me” reveals a small, charming wombat beside 80 blocks and up to 20 outlined blocks to express the range. Use a tidy abstract illustration, with no anatomical animation.

**Evidence:** Reported typical range, not a daily guarantee for every animal. The 2021 paper cites earlier work for the daily count; its own investigation concerns formation. The shapes are imperfect rectangular blocks, not exact geometric cubes. Secondary coverage says “each night” (wombats are nocturnal); confirm the paper's own wording before choosing “day” or “night” in the question.

**Source:** [Yang and colleagues, Soft Matter, 2021](https://hu.gatech.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/01/Hu21-wombat.pdf), journal pages 476–477; DOI 10.1039/D0SM01230K. Prize: [Ig Nobel winners list](https://improbable.com/ig/winners/), 2019 Physics. Review annually.

**Next connection:** WS-07, another animal doing something unexpectedly specific.

### WS-04 — A year before a turn

**Question:** “On Venus, which finishes first: one orbit or one full spin?”

**Reveal:** “The orbit: about 225 Earth days. The spin takes about 243.”

**Explanation:** “Venus travels around the Sun before it completes one rotation relative to the distant stars.”

**Visual and interaction:** Choose “Orbit” or “Spin,” or reveal immediately. Two progress rings advance against the same elapsed-time label. At 225 days, the orbit completes while the rotation remains unfinished. A fixed orientation mark makes the spin legible.

**Evidence:** Rounded astronomical periods. Say “spin” or “rotation,” not an unqualified “day”: the sunrise-to-sunrise period is different. Avoid recreating an orbit simulation unless its direction and reference frame are correct; labeled progress rings suffice. Optional second beat from the same NASA page: Venus also “spins the opposite direction of Earth and most other planets.” Use it only if it doesn't crowd the main reveal.

**Source:** [NASA, All About Venus](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/all-about-venus/en/), Time on Venus; corroborated by [NASA Venus Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/venusfact.html). Review annually.

**Next connection:** WS-08, a different way that sunrise and elapsed time diverge.

### WS-06 — Fifty-two cards, sixty-eight digits

**Question:** “How many different orders can a deck of 52 cards have?”

**Reveal:** “About 8.07 × 10⁶⁷. Written out, it's 68 digits.”

**Explanation:** “There are 52 choices for the first position, then 51 for the next, and so on. Multiply them together and the possibilities grow very quickly.”

**Visual and interaction:** Reveal the full number, typeset in readable groups. A comparison control shows 3 cards → 6 orders, 5 → 120, and 52 → the enormous total. The three-card state can show all six arrangements. Do not make the visitor wait through 52 steps.

**Evidence:** Exact calculation, assuming distinct cards, no jokers, and order matters: `52! = 80658175170943878571660636856403766975289505440883277824000000000000`. Recomputed locally. Do not claim a particular shuffle has certainly never occurred before.

**Source:** [John Baez, Mathematics of Games, 2013](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/games/games_10.html), Permutations, Example 2. No recurring data refresh needed.

**Next connection:** WS-12, multiplication that outruns intuition.

### WS-08 — Sixteen mornings

**Question:** “How many sunrises fit into 24 hours aboard the space station?”

**Reveal:** “About 16.”

**Explanation:** “The International Space Station circles Earth roughly every 90 minutes, giving it repeated passages between daylight and darkness.”

**Visual and interaction:** Reveal a ribbon of sixteen small dawns. Use a labeled typical-orbit illustration, with a static reduced-motion version.

**Evidence:** NASA's rounded average, not an exact count guaranteed on every date or a claim that astronauts personally watch every sunrise. Label “about” and “per 24 hours.”

**Source:** [NASA, Star Trails Seen From Low Earth Orbit](https://www.nasa.gov/image-article/star-trails-seen-from-low-earth-orbit/), October 7, 2016. Review annually while the station operates.

**Next connection:** WS-04, planetary time. Reserve because two space-time entries would be repetitive in a six-entry prototype.

### WS-12 — Forty-two doublings

**Question:** “If paper could keep doubling in thickness, when would it pass the distance to the Moon?”

**Reveal:** “After 42 doublings—starting at 0.1 millimeter.”

**Explanation:** “In this mathematical model, the thickness reaches about 439,805 kilometers. The Moon's average distance from Earth's center is about 384,400 kilometers.”

**Visual and interaction:** An adjustable doubling count with labeled milestones and an explicit change of scale. Let visitors jump to the reveal. Label “Thought experiment” before interaction.

**Evidence:** Hypothetical model, not a physically achievable folding task. `0.1 mm × 2⁴² ÷ 1,000,000 = 439,804.651 km`; 41 doublings give 219,902.326 km. Paper thickness is a chosen assumption. Distance is an average between centers, not a precise ground-to-lunar-surface journey. Both powers were recomputed locally.

**Source:** [NASA, Moon Facts](https://science.nasa.gov/moon/facts/), Size and Distance, supplies the comparison distance; the doubling calculation is ours. No live refresh needed.

**Next connection:** WS-06. Reserve because its familiar framing and invented setup may feel less distinctive than observed discoveries.

## Editorial decisions and remaining work

**Reader-check status, October 4:** [one walkthrough with the project's creator is complete](2026-10-04-reader-walkthrough.md). It records familiarity, stated interest, unanswered questions, and recommended next tests. Independent readers and the visual experience remain untested; the shortlist has not been validated.

- **Familiarity comes before production.** Show questions and reveals without polished artwork. Ask separately: “Did you already know this?”, “Did the answer change what you expected?”, and “Is it worth telling someone?” Record whether the underlying discovery is known, not just whether someone recalls the exact number.
- **Replace repeated misses.** If several intended readers recognize the central fact or find it uninteresting, replace it before building. These are qualitative editorial checks, not statistically representative novelty scores.
- **Keep the essential qualifier on the reveal.** “Modern U.S.,” “about,” “on foraging trips,” and the year of a documented project belong with the claim.
- **Research relationships and practices.** Prioritize institution records, study findings, object specifications, and useful comparisons derived from them. More obscure numbers alone do not make better entries.
- **Keep the six varied.** The new mix spans money, an animal's behavior, a local timekeeping tradition, art conservation, a building's movement, and mail delivery. The absence of a giant-number or astronomy entry is acceptable; a visual-format quota must not dictate content selection.
- **Do not quietly recycle the parked set.** Cloud weight, wombat cubes, Venus's rotation, and shuffle possibilities are excluded from the prototype after explicit user feedback. The sunrise and folded-paper entries are also parked because they risk the same problem. WS-07 and WS-10 remain unapproved reserves with their own familiarity risk.
- **Review visuals for truthfulness.** Preserve units, proportions, ranges, reference frames, and uncertainty. A correct number can still be drawn misleadingly.
- **Next deliverable:** two tracks before any full entry design. First, visual studies for WS-14 (pixel scale), WS-09 (mule route), and WS-13 (sleep spiral), each working static and in motion. Second, an independent plain-text check with fixed questions, a rotating first entry, recorded prompts, and the WS-13 and WS-11 opening variants tested alongside the originals. Choose the opener after both, following the [V1 brief](../v1-brief.md). No production interface has been built in this pass.

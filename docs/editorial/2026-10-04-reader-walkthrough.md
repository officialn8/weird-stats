## What this supports

All findings below are for one participant who had already seen the answers.

- Familiarity and appeal are separate. The coin and mule entries were familiar but still invited engagement; neither establishes novelty for this participant.
- For this participant, the seal and Oxford openings lost attention before the discovery. The seal question lacked curiosity; Oxford assumed recognition of a named bell. These are different wording problems, and they need testing with readers who do not know the answers.
- Unfamiliarity alone did not establish appeal for this participant. Oxford earned mild interest after explanation; the Eiffel Tower prompted doubt about significance.
- For this participant, the museum question earned attention without requiring prior knowledge of the painting. Its visual payoff remains untested.
- The participant considers design and motion central to the intended experience. A plain-text check can screen recognition and question clarity, but cannot settle the appeal of a visual explanation.

## Recommended next test — not a settled shortlist change

Make small visual studies for the painting's pixel scale, the mule route, and the seal's sleep spiral. Together they test microscopic scale, a spatial story, and motion that explains behavior. The painting earned curiosity and was new to this participant, and the mule route earned curiosity despite familiarity. The seal is included because its strongest asset, the spiral, cannot be judged in plain text, and its place as opener depends on it. The copper comparison is left out because it already worked in text and needs only two bars. Keep each study understandable in a static state as well as with motion.

Revise the seal opening before treating it as the landing-page lead. Try introducing the underwater sleeping mechanism as the source of curiosity; do not assume a wording change will succeed. Give Oxford's local-time tradition context before naming Great Tom. Candidate openings for both are recorded as variants in the [editorial selection](first-selection.md). Hold the Eiffel Tower entry for comparison with stronger candidates rather than spending on a finished animation yet. None of these recommendations permanently removes an entry based on one reader.

Run the lightweight text check with a few independent intended readers who have not seen the selection, then compare the visual studies with their static states. Test the revised seal and Oxford openings alongside the originals. Record prior regional or subject familiarity when volunteered.

- **Ask the same three questions every time:** “Did you already know this?”, “Did the answer change what you expected?”, and “Would you tell someone about it?” Leave unanswered fields unknown.
- **Rotate which entry comes first,** so no single entry is always judged cold, without a warm-up.
- **Record the exact prompts shown** to each reader, so responses can be compared across readers and wording versions.
- **Give the reader room to react** before explaining why an entry was chosen.

Choose the opening entry only after both the visual studies and the independent text check.

This walkthrough does not establish broad novelty, demand, retention, or a winning opener. No synthetic participant responses or audience percentages were used. The original shortlist remains provisional.

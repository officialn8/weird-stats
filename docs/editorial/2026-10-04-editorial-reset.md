# A stronger reason to keep scrolling

Created and sources checked: October 4, 2026. Status: the creator's walkthrough of the four new candidates is complete in the [second reader check](2026-10-04-second-reader-check.md). Chips earned the strongest articulated interest; printers remain a secondary candidate; ice cream is lower priority; the selfie opening was skipped. All three revealed topics were recalled, with tentative interest in seeing more. Independent responses remain uncollected.

## What changed

The creator's walkthrough separated novelty from interest: an unfamiliar fact could still feel inconsequential. Subsequent website feedback, quoted by the creator from his girlfriend, described familiar or irrelevant facts, little emotional weight, and an attractive but bland presentation. That is one reported whole-site reaction, not a recorded per-entry test. We do not know which facts she already knew.

The next experiment is whether everyday discoveries produce stronger curiosity and a specific desire to retell them. The existing copper, painting, and mule prototype remains a visual reference. Its current polish does not establish that those entries should lead the collection.

Keep the continuous scroll, orange identity, physical objects, and fluid motion. Select new material before making another elaborate scene. Everyday relevance is a search strategy for this round, not a permanent restriction on the site's subjects.

## The selection rule

An entry should start with something recognizable, overturn an understandable expectation, and leave the reader with a thought they can tell someone in one sentence. The number must clarify that discovery. Neither a large number nor a obscure subject earns a place by itself.

“Gravity” can mean amusement, recognition, disbelief, or a small change in how someone sees an ordinary thing. It does not require danger or outrage. Preserve the playful tone.

## Four candidates

The order below is my editorial preference for testing, not measured audience preference. Reader-facing copy is fixed in the [second reader check](2026-10-04-second-reader-check.md).

### WS-15 — The crunch changes the chip

**Opening:** “Could a volume knob make a potato chip seem fresher?”

**Evidence:** Zampini and Spence's 2004 experiment altered the biting sounds participants heard through headphones. Louder or enhanced high-frequency feedback increased perceived crispness and freshness. In informal questioning afterward, **15 of 20** thought the chips came from different packages, although they came from the same package.

**Scope:** A small experiment, with the package response collected informally afterward. Do not turn 15/20 into a general “75% of people can be fooled” claim or imply actual freshness changed.

**Sources:** [Oxford's original-study abstract](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publication/4405); [original paper, pp. 349–350 and 357, mirrored PDF](https://wiki.idiot.io/_media/zampini2004.pdf); DOI [10.1111/j.1745-459x.2004.080403.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-459x.2004.080403.x).

**Editorial bet:** A familiar snack gets an unexpected control. Familiarity is untested; the underlying experiment has received public coverage.

**Possible treatment:** A chip and an optional sound comparison. Label a browser demonstration illustrative: listening alone does not reproduce biting with altered feedback. The silent reveal must stand on its own.

**Connection:** Ice cream, where texture changes through a physical ingredient rather than sound.

### WS-16 — The camera distance changes the face

**Opening:** “Same face. No filter. What changes when the camera moves closer?”

**Evidence:** A 2018 geometry model estimated about **30% greater apparent nose width relative to face width at 12 inches**, compared with its undistorted reference projection. It modeled average facial measurements; it did not measure a universal effect across people's selfies.

**Source:** [Ward, Ward, Fried and Paskhover, original research letter, p. 2](https://www.ohadf.com/papers/WardWardFriedPaskhover_JAMA2018.pdf), DOI 10.1001/jamafacial.2018.0009.

**Qualification:** The paper's numerical baseline is an orthographic projection, effectively a camera infinitely far away. Do not describe 30% as an exact difference between 12-inch and five-foot photographs. Camera angle and individual geometry matter.

**Editorial bet:** A recognizable experience with a personal explanation. High familiarity risk among photography and social-media audiences. Avoid treating any face or proportion as defective.

**Possible treatment:** Move a camera around one fixed, original 3D head and show the resulting projection. Label it illustrative unless it implements the cited model. A generative face morph would not demonstrate the geometry reliably.

**Connection:** The chip experiment: two ways perception changes while the subject stays similar.

### WS-17 — A scoop with an invisible ingredient

**Opening:** “How much of a scoop of ice cream could be air?”

**Reveal:** “As much as half its volume. Air bubbles help give ice cream its texture; the amount varies.”

**Evidence:** ACS's educational account gives **30–50% of total volume**. Guelph's ice-cream technology text defines overrun as volume increase relative to the starting mix. In its 100% overrun example, the finished volume doubles: air accounts for half the resulting volume under that calculation.

**Sources:** [ACS, Ice, Cream… and Chemistry, p. 1](https://www.acs.org/content/dam/acsorg/education/resources/highschool/chemmatters/archive/chemmatters-feb2014-ice-cream.pdf); [University of Guelph, overrun calculations](https://books.lib.uoguelph.ca/icecreamtechnologyebook/chapter/overrun-calculations/).

**Qualification:** This is volume, not weight. Do not claim every scoop is half air, call air a scam, or equate 50% overrun with 50% air. A melting animation would also involve changes to the ice structure, so it would not isolate air content.

**Editorial bet:** The most immediate physical visual, but possibly familiar or unsurprising. Test before investing.

**Possible treatment:** Separate an illustrative scoop into labeled mix and air volumes, with no suggestion of a measured brand sample.

**Connection:** Copper: familiar objects whose appearance conceals their composition. Separate the two food entries in the eventual feed.

### WS-18 — The page has another layer

**Opening:** “Could a printed page reveal which printer made it—even without a name on it?”

**Evidence:** EFF decoded repeated **15 × 8** yellow-dot grids from Xerox DocuColor color printouts. The pattern carries a printer serial number and date/time information. Magnification or blue illumination makes the low-contrast marks easier to see.

**Source:** [EFF's original DocuColor decoding guide](https://w2.eff.org/Privacy/printers/docucolor/).

**Qualification:** Historical, model-specific evidence. The stored clock can be wrong. This does not establish that every current printer marks pages, identify the person who printed one, or show live location tracking. The grid has 120 positions, not necessarily 120 printed dots.

**Editorial bet:** A strong hidden-layer reveal, with weaker relevance for people who rarely print. The grid dimension supports the explanation; it is not itself an impressive statistic. Include only if the mechanism earns interest without inflated numerical framing.

**Possible treatment:** Sweep a blue light across an illustrated page to reveal a labeled example code. Show an explicit tap alternative and identify any invented sample data.

**Connection:** Selfies: ordinary images contain more than first meets the eye.

## Decision after the check

Use the four candidates plus copper as a familiar baseline with 3–5 independent readers. This is a practical qualitative screen, not an audience estimate. Record the actual retelling and whether the reader wants another entry, alongside familiarity and interest. The creator's reactions can guide copy but stay separate from independent-reader evidence.

Advance at most two entries to small visual treatments when multiple readers understand the claim, find the underlying idea new, and can articulate why they would tell a particular person. If responses are mostly polite approval or require us to explain why the fact matters, replace it. Revise wording when readers care but misunderstand. Check all claims again before publication, and assess the finished visual experience separately.

The next visual round should make the discovered mechanism tangible, use orange as the identity with contrast and breathing room, and vary the pacing between entries. Motion earns attention through a visible change, a clear payoff, and a glimpse of the next discovery. No candidate is yet approved as the homepage opener.

The [AAFPRS-authored release of January 26, 2017](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/aafprs-annual-survey-unveils-rising-trends-in-facial-plastic-surgery-300396391.html) reports that 42% of surgeons in its 2016 member survey saw patients seeking cosmetic procedures to look better in selfies or other social channels. This is a historical surgeon-response percentage, not a patient prevalence or causal finding.

Reopened [EFF’s decoding guide](https://w2.eff.org/Privacy/printers/docucolor/) to verify that blue illumination makes the yellow marks appear dark and magnification can help. Version 2 now includes that observable mechanism and the limit that machine identifiers do not identify the person who printed a page.

Verified [21 CFR 135.110(a)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-135.110): the U.S. ice-cream standard includes a 4.5-pound-per-gallon minimum. This is a weight-per-volume requirement, not a universal air percentage or a rule for every frozen dessert. These additions are sourced context for untested copy, not new reader-validation results.
